## Content / 内容

Given a query point $\mathbf{x}$ and a labelled training set $\{(\mathbf{x}_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, the **1-nearest-neighbour rule** simply returns the label of the training point that minimises a chosen distance:
$$
\hat{y}(\mathbf{x}) = y_{i^*},\qquad i^* = \arg\min_i\; d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_i).
$$
The most common distances are induced by the norms already studied:
$$
d_2(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|_2,\quad
d_1(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|_1,\quad
d_\infty(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|_\infty.
$$
Cosine distance $1-\frac{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_2\|\mathbf{z}\|_2}$ is preferred when only the angle matters (e.g. bag-of-words vectors).

The decision regions of 1-NN are exactly the **Voronoi cells** of the training points: every point closer to $\mathbf{x}_i$ than to any other training point receives label $y_i$. Consequently the decision boundary is a piecewise-linear (or piecewise-quadratic) surface whose complexity grows with the number of training samples.

给定查询点，1-NN 返回距离最近的训练样本的标签。决策区域就是训练点的 Voronoi 图。不同范数会产生不同的最近邻，因此也会产生不同的决策边界。

**Why the math of M0.1–M0.2 is indispensable**  
Without a clear notion of inner product and norm one cannot even write the distance, let alone understand why changing from $\ell_2$ to $\ell_1$ can flip a classification.

## Geometric picture / 几何直观

Imagine three training points of two classes in the plane. The perpendicular bisectors between every pair of points from different classes form the decision boundary. Adding a fourth point inserts new bisectors and can dramatically reshape the regions—an early warning of the method’s sensitivity to noise.

## Tiny Python sketch
```python
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors
import numpy as np
X = np.array([[0,0],[1,1],[2,0]])
nn = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=1, metric='euclidean').fit(X)
dist, idx = nn.kneighbors([[1,0]])
print(idx)   # which training point is nearest to (1,0)
```

## Practice / 练习

Training set: $(0,0)$ label A, $(2,0)$ label B, $(1,1)$ label A.  
What label does 1-NN with Euclidean distance assign to the query $(1,0.1)$?  
What changes if we switch to Manhattan distance?

<details>
<summary>Solution sketch</summary>

Euclidean distances: to $(0,0)\approx1.005$, to $(2,0)\approx1.005$, to $(1,1)\approx0.9$. Nearest is $(1,1)\to$ A.  
Manhattan: to $(0,0)=1.1$, to $(2,0)=1.1$, to $(1,1)=0.9$. Still A.  
(If the query were $(1,0)$, both norms make the three points equidistant—tie-breaking rule needed.)

</details>
