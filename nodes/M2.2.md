# M2.2 Normal Equations & Closed-Form Solution

## Content / 内容

Because $J(\mathbf{w})=\frac12\|X\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}\|_2^2$ is a convex quadratic (its Hessian $X^\top X$ is positive semi-definite), any point where the gradient vanishes is a global minimiser.  
From the matrix-calculus identity in [M0.8](M0.8.md):
$$
\nabla J = X^\top(X\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}).
$$
Setting the gradient to zero produces the **normal equations**
$$
X^\top X\,\mathbf{w} = X^\top\mathbf{y}.
$$

When $X$ has full column rank, $X^\top X$ is invertible and the unique solution is
$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}.
$$
If the columns of $X$ are linearly dependent (duplicate features, too many polynomial terms, …) then $X^\top X$ is singular. In that case one uses the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse; among all minimisers the pseudo-inverse solution has the smallest Euclidean norm.

因为平方损失是凸二次函数，梯度为零的点就是全局最小。令 $\nabla J=0$ 得到正规方程。满列秩时闭式解唯一；否则需伪逆。

### Geometric meaning (the picture you should keep)

$X\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ is the orthogonal projection of the vector $\mathbf{y}$ onto the column space of $X$. Consequently the residual $\mathbf{y}-X\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ is orthogonal to every column of $X$:
$$
X^\top(\mathbf{y}-X\hat{\mathbf{w}})=\mathbf{0},
$$
which is exactly the normal equations again. “Normal” here means “orthogonal”.

### Worked numeric example

Three points $(1,2)$, $(2,3)$, $(3,5)$ with intercept term:
$$
X=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\\1&3\end{pmatrix},\quad
\mathbf{y}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\\5\end{pmatrix}.
$$
$$
X^\top X=\begin{pmatrix}3&6\\6&14\end{pmatrix},\quad
X^\top\mathbf{y}=\begin{pmatrix}10\\23\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Solving the $2\times2$ system yields $\hat{w}_0=\frac23$, $\hat{w}_1=\frac32$.  
The fitted line is $y=\frac23+\frac32 x$.

## Tiny Python sketch
```python
w_hat = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
# or, when you know the matrix is full-rank:
w_hat = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)
```

## Common pitfalls / 易错点

- Forgetting the intercept column of ones → the hyperplane is forced through the origin.
- Using `np.linalg.inv(X.T@X)@X.T@y` instead of a solve/lstsq routine (numerically less stable).
- Interpreting a singular $X^\top X$ as “no solution”; there are infinitely many solutions, the pseudo-inverse picks the shortest one.
